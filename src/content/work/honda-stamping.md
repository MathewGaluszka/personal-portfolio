As a part of the Co-op Program at McMaster, I spent 16 months as an Electrical Engineering Intern at Honda of Canada Manufacturing in their Stamping department. Though I was hired as an electrical intern, thanks to the background experience of my degree I was able to take part in various mechanical, electrical, and software projects. I worked through the design, fabrication, installation and verification of both mechanical and electrical equipment projects, furthering my technical abilities as an engineer, and took part in the E-Flow process, gaining project management skills as a Project Leader of a couple projects. Below are 3 of the main projects that I took part in as a part of my time at HCM.

## Push Pull Machine

By far, the most difficult yet rewarding project that I worked on was being the Project Lead for a new Push Pull Machine that was being installed in the department. This machine would be utilized to perform an automated, standardized test that gauges how much force is required to move the part racks in the department. Starting out, I was tasked with providing electrical support for the project. I was responsible for checking, updating and finishing the electrical drawings. Issues were found in the drawings such as some breakers not being code compliant and a breaker being underspecified due to an incorrect current calculation. Once the drawings were corrected and finished, I then built the panel from scratch and completed an ESA inspection. The inspection revealed no issues with the wiring; however, a couple of panel labels needed to be changed and were rectified following the inspection. 

Following the electrical installation, the mechanical assembly was to be installed as well, however the project ended up stalling out as there was no budget allocated for a pit in the floor. This pit was necessary to prevent interference between the machine and the racks, and would also anchor the machine in one spot with no ability to move it in the future. Eventually after a workload rebalance, I was assigned to be the new Project Lead for this project, so I began to review the mechanical assembly for installation. This, however, led to the discovery of the linear rails being too short for the test area required. The test required an 8 ft length, but the linear rails already ordered were only 7ft 4in. I attempted to salvage them through optimizing acceleration/deceleration through dynamics calculations and kinematic equations, however it was still shown that the existing setup would be insufficient for use, especially when extra travel was required for soft limits and other safety requirements. To overcome this, I had a discussion with my supervisor, and we determined the best path forward was to take a step backwards and re-evaluate the project.

First, I began by meeting with stakeholders to re-define project requirements and make sure that they are updated to reflect what they want out of the system. From there, I began re-evaluating the current rack situation by calculating theoretical ergonomic values for all carts on both polyurethane and nylon casters. With this data, we then completed manual push pull tests to determine if consistently performed manual tests would correlate with theoretical calculations. This showed that around 70% of the manual tests fell within the theoretical value range, meaning that theoretical calculations can be used to roughly determine how a rack would perform. We also looked at the manpower requirements for the automated tester utilizing statistics calculations to determine sample sizes, and after estimating how long an automated test would take and hourly wages, we could determine the time and budget cost to running the machine.

Finally, a new mechanical assembly was required. I worked through the design process and built a new Catia model of a potential solution, taking inspiration from other equipment in the department, and determined the clearance height required for racks to be compatible. A new risk assessment was also completed for the design, and we also took measurements of all the racks in the department to see how many would be excluded or need to be modified, considering our sample sizes from earlier. Unfortunately, this was not all resolved before my time at HCM was done, but the next steps have been taken to review all this information with stakeholders and determine the path forward for the project.

## ActivPlant

Early on in my time at HCM, I took over as Stamping's ActivPlant Subject Matter Expert (SME). ActivPlant is the plant's system for automated production data and reporting. The previous SME had already left and no one had backfilled the role since, so the documentation I inherited was thin and there was little to no direction left. I started out by:

- Verifying that ALL production data collected (part counts, cycle time, etc.)  is correct and accurate by comparing to what is occurring lineside 
- Cross-referencing lineside HMI/PLC fault lists and ActivPlant Incident Descriptions to ensure they match and report downtime incidents correctly
- Re-checking the report used by Production associates, ensuring that the data is accurate and useful 
- Understanding possible gaps/data points that could be collected in the future

Much of this verification was done in Excel, using a variety of formulas and macros to complete the work most efficiently. Once errors were identified, there were many PLC logic changes that needed to be made to rectify the issues. Going through this re-validation of the systems increased the reliability of the data collected, and as a result the trust in the system from other associates in the department increased. At first, most of the issues were identified by myself, however most of the requests I attempted to fill by the end of my time at HCM came from associates within the department. By utilizing the voice of the floor and talking to associates, I was able to learn from them and address their concerns accordingly, as well as close some of the remaining gaps in the system. Thanks to a bunch of data analysis to understand what works and what needs improvements, troubleshooting logic and making numerous PLC and HMI programming changes to address the gaps identified by Production associates, the department went from having one of the lowest data integrity scores to the best score of all 12 main departments in HCM that utilized ActivPlant.

## Laser Gantry Replacement

One of the 4 main lines in the Stamping department was the Laser Line, which would weld 2 panels of sheet metal together with a high power laser. This gantry replacement project was initiated because an end-of-life linear encoder that was mounted on the previous laser gantry needed to be replaced, and the new encoder chosen had different bolt patterns. On top of that, new linear rails and linear motors were going to be installed, and those also had different mounting styles. This meant that the entire gantry assembly and carriage needed to be replaced to fit the new components.

I provided mechanical support on this project, and because the existing gantry did not have CAD files, the existing paper drawings needed to be translated into Catia V5 parts and assemblies. Of course, this translation was not just a carbon-copy, certain parts needed to be modified to accommodate the new parts, such as (but not limited to):
- Redesigning the front plate of the static assembly to reduce fabrication requirements
- Adjusting pocket depths to ensure correct spacing between the primary/secondary magnets of the linear motor
- Re-tolerancing all mounts for the linear motor, encoder and rails according to manufacturer's specifications
- Adjusting bolt patterns for new drag chain mount
	
By the end of the re-specification, there were 4 sub-assemblies made (Gantry, Carriage, Horizontal Axis, Cable Tray) and a total of 22 unique parts. These parts were then turned into drawings and sent out to machine shops to quote and eventually order once a shop was chosen. Upon delivery, the components were assembled in a temporary location to validate form and function leading into shutdown. Overall, there were no major errors with the mechanical assembly and the gantry functioned as intended, however minor modifications (small cutouts in the carriage side plates) needed to be made to ensure proper clearance for auxiliary parts that were last-minute requirements. These were reflected in the drawings accordingly. Through this project, I learned how to approach mechanical design/redesign for steel and aluminum components, including GD&T dimensioning and tolerancing, weld specification, DFM/DFA considerations, and sheet metal design. It also reinforced my CAD design abilities through real-world application.
