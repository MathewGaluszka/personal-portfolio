One of the first technical jobs that I worked was at Canada's Wonderland as a Rides Electrical Technician. I worked there for 2 summers between first and second year and second and third year, and it was this job that first exposed me to working with industrial electrical systems. As a seasonal employee, I was tasked with working on and looking after the 25 rides in the kids section of the park, but I would also help out the full-time electricians at the rest of the rides within the park.

## Daily Inspection and Preventative Maintenance
Days typically consisted of two sections, before park open and park operation. Before park open, all rides have a certain daily inspection checklist and test cycles that need to be completed before they could open to the public that day, so early mornings consisted of running through those inspections and handing the rides off to operations. This was also an opportunity to complete weekly/monthly preventative maintenance on rides that required more work on a less regular schedule. In the event I found an issue with the ride, this would be the time to troubleshoot the error and attempt to get it ready to run on time for park open, which was attainable most of the time barring some major work that needed to be done. Shifts started at 6 and OPS would need the rides for their own pre-open checks by 9\:30, so those first three and a half hours were spent going around and verifying that everything should operate correctly that day and working on any backlog of minor fixes that were noted previosuly, such as lights not working or units being down.

## On-Call During Park Operation
Once the park opened, the job switched from performing routine preventative maintenance to being on-call and responding to ride downtimes. Of course, even with all the inspections in the world, rides would still break down. In that case, I would respond to radio downtime calls and attempt to troubleshoot the issue. Some were downtime calls were super simple, operator panel buttons not lighting up or cars not dispatching, while some could be real head-scratchers. Regardless, there was a 15 minute clock that starts from the first downtime call before the downtime gets escalated to higher-ups, so efficient work was a necessity. I quickly learned the most effective strategies to troubleshoot issues and identify root causes in order to get the rides back up and running as fast as possible for the guests.

## Overlap and Park Close
That would be the end of the day if you were on day shift, but afternoons worked different. I would come in and overlap with the other seasonal working the day shift, going over the issues of the day so far and any reoccuring issue that may arise again, and then take over on-call duty to let them go home. Once the park closed, closing checks would occur and all the rides would be put away for the night, preparing for the next morning. In the event there was a major downtime on a ride, this would be the time to try and get it up and running again, even if it meant I was there into the wee hours of the morning. This would also be the time for any other large jobs that required doing, such as retrofits or rewires that needed to be completed.

## Skills Learned
Thanks to the variety of rides in the park that ranged from brand new to 45 years old, I got exposure to all the different electrical control systems under the sun and learned how to work with them accordingly. This job also taught me how to think quick on my feet and work under pressure, but most importantly it taught me how to ask the right questions and learn from the full-time staff that had many more years of experience than I had.
